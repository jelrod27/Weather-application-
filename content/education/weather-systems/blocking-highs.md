A blocking high is the reason two places 1,500 miles apart can spend the same fortnight in opposite weather — one under cloudless heat with no rain in the forecast, the other taking rain band after rain band on the same track. Both are symptoms of a single feature in the mid-level flow that has stopped moving.

## The pattern that stops the traffic

Weather in the mid-latitudes moves west to east because the westerly winds aloft move west to east. Those winds are not a straight belt; they run in large wavy patterns called Rossby waves, or longwaves, discovered by Carl Rossby in the 1930s. A single longwave runs from about 3,700 miles to 5,000 miles crest-to-crest, and at any moment the hemisphere carries somewhere between three and seven of them, most often four or five. Surface lows and their fronts ride along inside this pattern like cars on a road.

The longwaves themselves drift east only slowly, and occasionally they stop or retrograde — move east to west. A block is the extreme case of that stall. Centers of high and low pressure aloft arrange themselves so that approaching systems cannot get through the usual way. They are deflected around the obstacle instead, and whatever weather is already in place under each center stays there. Blocks can hold for several days at a time.

The mechanism that builds the waves in the first place is geographic. The sun heats the Earth unevenly because land masses differ in shape and size, and air that meets a mountain range must climb over it or go around. Both disruptions leave imbalances in how temperature is distributed, horizontally and vertically. The wind responds by changing speed and direction, trying to restore balance — and because the sun keeps shining, the imbalance keeps regenerating. The waves are the atmosphere's permanent, never-quite-finished correction.

## Omega and Rex

Two configurations get named because they recur and because they behave differently.

An omega block is two cut-off lows with a single blocking high wedged between them. On a 500 mb chart the height contours trace out the Greek letter omega — a broad dome in the middle with a trailing low on each flank. Cut-off lows earn their own reputation ("cut-off low, weatherman's woe") because they have been pulled out of the main airflow entirely and drift for days. Forecast models handle their motion reasonably well but tend to have them open up and rejoin the flow aloft sooner than they actually do. Omega blocks are large, and their size is part of why they persist.

A Rex block is the simpler shape: a high sitting poleward of a low, stacked. That arrangement is nearly self-supporting. It will stay put until one of the two height centers changes intensity and unbalances the stack. Until then, unsettled and stormy weather sits under the low half and dry conditions sit under the high half.

The consequences follow the geometry. Under an omega block, cool temperatures and precipitation accompany the two lows while warm, clear conditions prevail under the high. Persistent examples of either type can produce flooding near the low-pressure part of the pattern and short-term drought under the high-pressure part — the same feature, the same days, two outcomes.

## Why the high half gets hot

High pressure aloft makes air subside. Sinking air is compressed, and compression warms it. That warming happens in the lower atmosphere, exactly where people live, and at the same time the subsidence caps heat rising from the surface instead of letting it escape upward. The two effects stack day after day, which is why blocking highs are responsible for major heat waves rather than merely warm afternoons. The clear skies are part of the same mechanism: sinking air suppresses cloud, so there is nothing to shade the ground, and the following day starts warmer than the last.

Whatever precipitation the pattern still carries gets shunted around the periphery of the high-pressure area — around the rim, never through the middle.

## Recognising one

The chart to look at is 500 mb. That surface sits between roughly 16,000 and nearly 20,000 feet, which is why it is treated as the middle of the atmosphere and why, if a forecaster could have only one map, it would be this one. The lines on it are height contours, given in meters. Height contours bulge poleward where the air below is warm and sag equatorward where it is cold, so the ridge of a blocking high shows up as a bulge in the contours. The chart prints these with the final zero dropped, so a contour labelled 564 marks 5,640 meters, about 18,503 feet.

A static chart is not enough to read a block reliably, because the smaller waves embedded in the longwave pattern — shortwaves, anything under 3,700 miles — need a loop to be distinguished. Shortwaves travel downstream at about 23 mph in summer and 35 mph in winter, and they are the chief instigator of precipitation episodes. Watch a multi-day loop of 500 mb heights: if the large-scale shape holds still while smaller disturbances slide around its edges, that is a block.

The ground-level version of the same observation needs no chart. If your forecast has looked identical for four consecutive days — same sky, same temperature trend, no front in sight — and the national radar shows storms repeatedly training over a region a thousand miles or more away, you are inside one half of a block and watching the other half work.

## How it breaks, and what to do while it holds

A blocking high weakens when a shortwave finally manages to move over the top of it, cutting the ridge down and ending the heat wave. A Rex block breaks when one of its two centers changes intensity. Neither is easy to time, and the known model bias on cut-off lows runs in the unhelpful direction: forecasts tend to release the pattern too early.

The practical hazard is not wind or a storm arriving. It is duration. Heat that would be tolerable for a day becomes dangerous when the sinking air renews it every afternoon for a week, and rainfall that a catchment can absorb once becomes flooding when the same track is used repeatedly. When a block is in place, the useful question is not what tomorrow looks like — tomorrow looks like today — but how many more days of today the ground, the grid and the people can absorb.
