On January 17, 1994, at 4:31 a.m. local time, a magnitude 6.7 earthquake ruptured beneath Northridge, California — a suburb of Los Angeles sitting directly above a blind thrust fault that appeared on no pre-event hazard map. Fifty-seven people died. Direct losses exceeded $20 billion. The fault itself never broke the surface. That invisibility is not unusual. It is the rule.

![Global map of earthquake epicenters from 1963 to 1998, tracing the world's tectonic plate boundaries.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Quake_epicenters_1963-98.png?width=1280)

Understanding why requires looking past the shaking and into the mechanics of what earthquakes actually are, what they reveal about Earth's interior, and why two quakes with identical magnitudes can produce radically different outcomes.

## What an Earthquake Actually Measures

An earthquake is the sudden release of elastic strain energy stored along a fault — a fracture in the crust where two blocks of rock are locked together by friction while tectonic forces slowly load them like a compressed spring. When friction loses to stress, the fault ruptures. The rupture propagates along the fault plane at roughly 2–3 kilometers per second, radiating energy outward as seismic waves.

Magnitude is a measure of that energy release, but the scale is logarithmic in a way that routinely surprises people: each whole-number step represents approximately 31.6 times more energy. A magnitude 7.0 releases about 1,000 times the energy of a magnitude 5.0. The moment magnitude scale (Mw), which USGS now uses as the standard, is anchored to the physical dimensions of the rupture — fault area, average slip, and the rigidity of the rock — rather than to the amplitude of a single instrument reading.

Depth matters as much as magnitude. A magnitude 6.0 at 5 kilometers depth can be catastrophic directly above the rupture; the same magnitude at 300 kilometers depth — a common occurrence in subducting slabs — may be felt across an entire continent but cause no structural damage anywhere. The 2013 magnitude 8.3 Sea of Okhotsk earthquake ruptured at 609 kilometers depth, making it one of the deepest large earthquakes ever recorded. It was felt as far away as Moscow, roughly 7,000 kilometers distant, yet caused no significant damage.

## Focal Mechanisms and Why They Matter

The focal mechanism — sometimes called the "beachball diagram" in seismology shorthand — describes the geometry of the fault and the direction of slip. It is derived from the first-motion patterns of P-waves arriving at seismic stations around the world, and it encodes whether a fault is strike-slip (horizontal motion, like the San Andreas), normal (crust pulling apart), or thrust/reverse (crust compressing, one block riding over another).

That geometry determines tsunami potential more directly than magnitude alone. Thrust faults on subduction zones — where one tectonic plate dives beneath another — can displace the seafloor vertically across hundreds of kilometers. That vertical displacement is what drives a tsunami. For the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (Mw 9.1), a [USGS review of rupture models](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2013/1170/b/pdf/of2013-1170b_text.pdf) reports average-slip estimates of 12–25 meters across models; the [USGS event summary](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/eventpage/official20110311054624120_30) describes maximum modeled slip of 50–60 meters. The rupture displaced the seafloor and the water column above it. Strike-slip earthquakes, even large ones, rarely generate significant tsunamis because the dominant motion is horizontal.

This is why the USGS and NOAA tsunami warning centers evaluate focal mechanism and tectonic setting, not just magnitude, when issuing warnings within minutes of a large submarine earthquake.

## Aftershocks and the Decay Sequence

The largest earthquake in any sequence is called the mainshock. Every earthquake modifies the stress field around it, and those modifications trigger additional ruptures — aftershocks — that follow a remarkably consistent statistical pattern described by Omori's Law: aftershock rate decays roughly as the inverse of time since the mainshock. Double the elapsed time, roughly halve the rate.

What surprises most people is that aftershocks can themselves be damaging, and that the largest aftershock in a sequence is typically about one magnitude unit smaller than the mainshock — meaning a magnitude 7.0 mainshock is expected to produce a magnitude 6.0 largest aftershock. That aftershock hits structures already compromised by the mainshock, which is why building collapse in major earthquakes frequently occurs during the aftershock sequence rather than the initial rupture.

![Smoldering ruins along Market Street in San Francisco after the April 18, 1906 earthquake and fire.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Sanfranciscoearthquake1906.jpg?width=1280)
*U.S. National Archives (ARC 531006)*

There is no reliable way to predict when aftershock sequences end. Some sequences decay to background seismicity within weeks. The 1992 Landers, California sequence produced a felt aftershock more than two years after the mainshock.

## Eyes on the Sky

- If you live near a mapped fault zone, look up your address on the USGS Earthquake Hazards Program site (earthquake.usgs.gov) and check both the fault distance and the site soil class — soft sediment amplifies shaking by a factor of 2 to 5 compared to bedrock at the same distance.
- After any felt earthquake, check USGS Did You Feel It? and submit a report; the crowdsourced intensity data meaningfully improves ShakeMap accuracy for smaller events where station coverage is sparse.
- Tsunamis can travel inland along [rivers and streams connected to the ocean](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/tsunamis/tsunami-inundation). Follow local evacuation-zone maps and instructions. If you are near the coast and feel strong or prolonged shaking, protect yourself during the earthquake, then [move quickly to high ground or inland, away from the water, without waiting for an official warning](https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/prep-you).
