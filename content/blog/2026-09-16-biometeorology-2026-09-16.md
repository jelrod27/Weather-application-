Harris County, Texas, contains 4.7 million people, a refinery corridor that runs for 25 miles along the Ship Channel, and a flood-plain footprint so extensive that roughly 800,000 residents live in a designated 100-year floodplain. The county's ongoing climate-risk mapping effort is doing something that weather forecasters have rarely been asked to do: it is connecting atmospheric hazards directly to health burden, neighborhood by neighborhood, and asking which populations sit at the intersection of the most exposures at once. That question is the operational core of biometeorology — the scientific field that treats the atmosphere not as a backdrop but as a direct driver of biological outcomes.

## The Body Keeps Score of the Forecast

Biometeorology has been a formal discipline since the 1950s, but its applications have accelerated sharply as climate signals become legible at the scale of a single zip code. The field covers territory that most people experience without naming: the reason your asthma flares on certain summer afternoons has a fluid-dynamic explanation, the reason bird migration windows are compressing has a phenological one, and the reason your mood reliably deteriorates in November has a photobiological one.

Heat illness is the most direct link. Wet-bulb temperature accounts for heat and humidity, but it does not define a single safe-versus-dangerous boundary. The often-cited 35°C wet-bulb value is a theoretical upper limit under idealized assumptions, not the point where serious heat risk begins. In [controlled research involving young, healthy adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34913738/), the mean critical wet-bulb temperature was approximately 30.55°C in warm, humid conditions during minimal activity, with lower limits in hotter, drier conditions. This critical limit marks the point where the body can no longer maintain heat balance; it is not a universal threshold for heat illness or death. Activity, exposure duration, age, health, and access to cooling all affect risk. For outdoor workers, people without stable housing, and residents without air conditioning, that risk is a direct physiological threat, not a statistical abstraction.

The county-level mapping layers heat exposure onto pre-existing health burden — rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and respiratory illness — and onto social indicators like housing age, tree canopy coverage, and access to cooling centers. The result is a vulnerability surface: a spatial picture of where the atmosphere's worst days land on bodies least equipped to absorb them.

## Thunderstorm Asthma and the Mechanics of Pollen Rupture

One of biometeorology's less intuitive findings is that thunderstorms can trigger mass asthma events in people who have never been diagnosed with asthma. The mechanism was worked out in detail following a catastrophic outbreak in Melbourne, Australia, in November 2016, when an epidemic thunderstorm asthma event contributed to 10 deaths. The [Victorian Department of Health](https://www.health.vic.gov.au/environmental-health/epidemic-thunderstorm-asthma-program) reports 3,365 more respiratory-related emergency-department presentations than expected at Melbourne and Geelong public hospitals in the 30 hours from 6 p.m. on November 21—a 672% increase over the three-year average.

The physics: rye grass pollen grains, which are too large to penetrate deep into the lung, absorb moisture at the leading edge of a thunderstorm's outflow and rupture into sub-particles small enough to reach the bronchioles. The outflow boundary simultaneously concentrates those particles near the surface just as wind drives them into a populated area. The result is an aerobiological event with the spatial coherence of a weather system and the clinical signature of a chemical exposure.

Houston's thunderstorm season overlaps with its peak Juniperus and Poaceae pollen season, and the city's Ship Channel corridor adds industrial particulates that interact with pollen particles in ways that amplify inflammatory response. Mapping that interaction — where storm tracks, pollen source regions, and high-sensitivity populations converge — is exactly the kind of compound-hazard analysis that Harris County's effort is attempting to formalize.

## Phenology as the Long Signal

Biometeorology also operates at timescales longer than a single storm. Phenology — the study of cyclical biological events and their relationship to climate — tracks the timing of first bloom, first freeze, bird arrival, and insect emergence. In the continental United States, the lilac first-bloom index, maintained since the 1950s, shows a consistent advance of roughly six days per decade across the northern tier.

That shift has consequences that compound. Earlier bloom means earlier and longer pollen seasons. The ragweed season in the central U.S. has extended by approximately 25 days since 1995, driven primarily by later first-frost dates. For the 26 million Americans with allergic rhinitis, that is not a calendar curiosity — it is an additional month of symptomatic exposure each year.

The same phenological shift that lengthens pollen seasons also disrupts the timing relationships between migratory species and their food sources, a mismatch problem ecologists call phenological decoupling. Neotropical songbirds time their northward migration to coincide with insect emergence, which is itself timed to leaf-out. When those events drift at different rates, the energetic cost of migration rises — and population-level effects follow.

## Looking Ahead

- Explore the CDC/ATSDR [Heat & Health Index](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/hhi/index.html), available through the Heat & Health Tracker, to compare heat vulnerability at the ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) level. These areas approximate ZIP codes; they are not census tracts. For finer local detail, check county public-health maps, and use the [U.S. Climate Resilience Toolkit](https://toolkit.climate.gov/) to find additional planning resources.
- Follow local heat alerts and public-health guidance during summer heat events. Wet-bulb temperature can provide context about heat and humidity, but no single reading guarantees safety or captures every factor affecting personal heat risk.
- Watch for thunderstorm asthma alerts in cities that combine high grass-pollen counts with active convective forecasts; Australia's Melbourne now issues them operationally, and U.S. cities with similar climatology are the logical next candidates.
