On November 25, 2026, the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory reported Aviation Color Code YELLOW for Kīlauea. The observatory had lowered the code from ORANGE on November 23 after lava fountaining ended at 6 a.m. Hawaiʻi Standard Time. During the pause, the summit began swelling again and the vents continued to release sulfur dioxide (SO2). [USGS volcano updates](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/volcano-updates) · [Episode 57 status report](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/notice/DOI-USGS-HVO-2026-11-23T17%3A33%3A15%2B00%3A00)

## How the Alert System Actually Works

YELLOW is the second of four USGS aviation color codes. After a downgrade from ORANGE, it means activity has eased and the observatory is watching for a renewed increase. Separately, nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centers (VAACs) monitor airborne ash around the clock. The Washington VAAC covers Hawaiʻi. When ash is detected or reported, the responsible center issues an advisory describing the cloud’s location, affected flight levels, and expected movement. Airlines, dispatchers, and air traffic controllers use that information to plan routes around the ash. [USGS alert levels](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/alert-level-system) · [National Weather Service VAAC guidance](https://www.weather.gov/aviation/vaac)

Volcanic ash is especially dangerous to jet engines. Ingested silicate particles can melt in hot sections of the engine and harden on cooler components, causing the engine to lose thrust. In 1982, a British Airways 747 flew into an ash cloud from Indonesia’s Galunggung volcano and lost power in all four engines at about 37,000 feet. The crew restarted three of them before landing in Jakarta. The aircraft suffered extensive damage. [USGS account of volcanic ash encounters](https://www.usgs.gov/congressional/statement-james-e-quick-program-coordinator-volcano-hazards-program-usgs)

Hawaiian shield volcanoes often produce fluid lava flows, and their gas emissions can create serious air-quality problems. USGS reprocessed its measurements from Kīlauea’s 2018 lower East Rift Zone eruption and estimated that the fissures emitted nearly 200,000 tonnes of SO2 per day during much of June and early July. Sulfur dioxide can react in the atmosphere to form vog, a haze that can affect air quality far downwind. [USGS revised emissions estimate](https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcano-watch-new-assessment-kilaueas-extreme-so2-emission-rates-2018) · [USGS volcanic gas hazards](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/volcanic-gases-can-be-harmful-health-vegetation-and-infrastructure)

## What SO2 Does Once It Leaves the Vent

Sulfur dioxide can affect climate as well as local air quality. When an explosive eruption sends SO2 above the tropopause, the boundary between the troposphere and stratosphere, the gas forms tiny sulfate droplets. The droplets scatter incoming sunlight back to space, which can cool Earth’s surface. [USGS overview of volcanic climate effects](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/vhp/volcanoes-can-affect-climate)

The benchmark case is Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines, which erupted in June 1991 and sent an estimated 20 million tonnes of SO2 into the stratosphere. The sulfate aerosols spread around the planet, and global temperatures fell by about 0.5°C for roughly two years. [USGS account of Pinatubo’s cooling](https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcano-watch-global-reach-volcanic-gases)

![Mount Pinatubo eruption column from Clark Air Base, June 1991.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Pinatubo91eruption_clark_air_base.jpg?width=1280)
*USGS*

The 1883 eruption of Krakatau, also known as Krakatoa, likewise produced a widespread stratospheric aerosol layer. The cooling depends in part on how much SO2 an eruption releases and how high the gas reaches. Gas from a smaller eruption can have a greater cooling effect if it reaches the stratosphere, above the part of the atmosphere where rain removes sulfate aerosols more quickly. [USGS analysis of volcanic cooling](https://www.usgs.gov/volcanoes/yellowstone/science/climate-change-potential-a-result-a-large-eruption-yellowstone)

![Mount St. Helens lateral blast and ash column, May 18, 1980.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/MSH80_eruption_mount_st_helens_05-18-80.jpg?width=1280)
*Austin Post / USGS*

The TROPOMI instrument aboard Copernicus Sentinel-5P maps how much SO2 is present in a column of air. Since August 6, 2019, its SO2 measurements have had a ground footprint of about 5.5 km by 3.5 km directly beneath the satellite. These observations help researchers model where volcanic gas travels. For a longer view, researchers use tree rings to reconstruct summer temperatures after historic eruptions. [TROPOMI observations of the Cumbre Vieja eruption](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/49821/noaa_49821_DS1.pdf) · [ECMWF on volcanic gas modeling](https://www.ecmwf.int/en/newsletter/171/news/monitoring-atmospheric-impacts-hunga-tonga-eruption) · [Tree-ring research on volcanic cooling](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10666123/)

## What to Watch

- Follow the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory’s Volcano Activity Notices and Volcano Observatory Notices for Aviation for alert changes and the observations behind them. The observatory weighs multiple measurements and the eruption’s history when changing a code. [USGS volcano notifications](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/) · [How USGS sets alert levels](https://www.usgs.gov/observatories/hvo/news/volcano-watch-what-are-volcano-alert-level-and-aviation-color-code)
- For airborne ash near Hawaiʻi, follow the Washington VAAC advisories and the Honolulu forecast office’s aviation warnings (SIGMETs). [National Weather Service Honolulu aviation guidance](https://www.weather.gov/aviation/hfo)
- After a major explosive eruption, use an SO2 column-density map to see where the gas spread. To assess whether the gas reached the stratosphere, compare a TROPOMI SO2 layer-height retrieval, where available, with the tropopause height at the plume’s location and observation time. [Satellite study of the Tonga eruption](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10973981/)
