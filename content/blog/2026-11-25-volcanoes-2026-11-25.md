On November 25, 2026, the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory issued an Aviation Color Code YELLOW for Kīlauea — the second tier in a four-level system that runs from GREEN through YELLOW, ORANGE, and RED. No lava fountaining, no ash column. Just elevated unrest: ground deformation, increased seismicity, and a sulfur dioxide flux that the observatory flagged as worth watching. It sounds routine. The machinery it activates is not.

## How the Alert System Actually Works

The moment a volcano crosses into YELLOW, it enters a monitoring posture shared by nine Volcanic Ash Advisory Centers worldwide. VAACs are designated by the International Civil Aviation Organization and operate around the clock. The Anchorage VAAC covers the North Pacific, including Hawaiʻi. When SO2 emissions or ash become significant enough to threaten flight corridors, the relevant VAAC issues a Volcanic Ash Advisory — a standardized product that specifies plume height, dispersion direction, and the flight levels affected. Airlines, dispatchers, and air traffic control receive these advisories and use them to reroute or hold aircraft.

The reason aviation authorities treat volcanic plumes with particular seriousness is a physics problem that jet engines cannot negotiate. Volcanic ash is not soft particulate. It is pulverized silicate rock — glass, essentially — with a melting point close to the operating temperature inside a turbine. Ash ingested by a jet engine softens, coats turbine blades, and can cause flameout. In 1982, a British Airways 747 flew into an ash cloud from Indonesia's Galunggala volcano and lost all four engines simultaneously at 37,000 feet. The crew restarted three of them before landing in Jakarta. The aircraft was effectively destroyed.

Kīlauea's current YELLOW status does not suggest that kind of event is imminent. Hawaiian shield volcanoes tend toward effusive eruptions — fluid lava flows rather than explosive columns. But effusive does not mean inert. During Kīlauea's 2018 lower East Rift Zone eruption, SO2 emission rates reached roughly 50,000 tonnes per day at peak, generating a regional air quality crisis that grounded small aircraft and issued health warnings across the island chain. The VAAC system was actively tracking SO2 dispersion throughout.

## What SO2 Does Once It Leaves the Vent

Sulfur dioxide's role in short-term air quality is well understood. Its role in long-term climate is less intuitive. When eruptions are explosive enough to inject SO2 above the tropopause — into the stratosphere, typically above 12 kilometers — the gas oxidizes and combines with water vapor to form sulfate aerosol droplets. These droplets scatter incoming solar radiation back to space. The effect is a measurable, sometimes dramatic, global cooling.

The benchmark case is Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines, which erupted in June 1991 and sent an estimated 20 million tonnes of SO2 into the stratosphere. The resulting sulfate aerosol layer persisted for roughly two years and reduced global mean surface temperature by approximately 0.5°C — enough to temporarily offset a decade of anthropogenic warming. The year 1992 was the coolest year of the 1980s and 1990s combined.

Krakatoa's 1883 eruption was larger in raw energy but injected less SO2 per unit of eruptive volume; the climate signal was real but shorter-lived. What matters for climate forcing is not eruption size alone but injection altitude and SO2 mass. A small explosive eruption that punches into the stratosphere can outperform a larger effusive eruption that stays in the troposphere, where rain scrubs sulfate aerosols out within days to weeks.

Modern satellites track this in near-real time. The Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite carries an instrument called Tropomi — the TROPOspheric Monitoring Instrument — which measures SO2 column density globally at 3.5-kilometer horizontal resolution. Within hours of a significant eruption, Tropomi data feeds into dispersion models used by both VAACs and climate scientists. The same SO2 plume that informs a flight rerouting decision over the Pacific also enters the volcanic forcing datasets used to validate climate projections.

Kīlauea's current emissions are not stratospheric. But the alert status is a reminder that the monitoring infrastructure connecting a vent on the Big Island to an aviation advisory in Anchorage to a climate model in Hamburg is continuous, automated, and genuinely integrated.

## What to Watch

- Track the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory's SO2 emission rate data, published daily when Kīlauea is active; a sustained jump above 2,000 tonnes per day typically precedes an escalation from YELLOW to ORANGE.
- Check the Anchorage VAAC's advisory feed if you have flights routing through the North Pacific — advisories are issued in near-real time and are publicly accessible through the ICAO Volcanic Ash Advisory Database.
- The next time a major explosive eruption occurs anywhere on the planet, look for Tropomi SO2 imagery within 24 hours; the KNMI data viewer publishes it publicly, and the difference between a tropospheric plume and a stratospheric one is visible in the column density maps.
