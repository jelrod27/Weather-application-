On November 11, 2026, two different weather products illustrated the range of aviation concerns: a Yellow aviation color code at Kīlauea and marginal severe thunderstorm risk in the lower Ohio and western Tennessee valleys and east-central Florida. The volcano update and storm outlook describe different kinds of potential hazards; neither establishes that a particular flight encountered a hazard or changed course. Understanding how those products differ from a SIGMET is part of reading aviation weather beyond the surface forecast.

## What a SIGMET Actually Tells You

A SIGMET — Significant Meteorological Information — advises of weather potentially hazardous to all aircraft. It describes the affected area, hazard, and validity period; vertical limits help crews assess exposure along a route. In the contiguous United States, convective SIGMETs address thunderstorm hazards, while nonconvective SIGMETs cover hazards including severe icing, severe or extreme turbulence, and volcanic ash. Different criteria apply to those products, but both land in the same preflight package a dispatcher reviews before signing off on a release. [Aviation Weather Center product guidance.](https://aviationweather.gov/gfa/help/?page=tutorial)

The November 11 USGS update, published at 08:25 HST (18:25 UTC), reported Kīlauea at volcano alert level Advisory and aviation color code Yellow. The Halemaʻumaʻu eruption was paused after episode 56 fountaining ended on November 5; a pause within an episodic eruption is different from saying there is no ongoing eruption. [USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, November 11, 2026.](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/notice/DOI-USGS-HVO-2026-11-11T17:28:12+00:00) The aviation color code communicates volcano status, not a routing instruction. Orange can indicate heightened unrest or an eruption with no or minor ash emissions. [USGS alert-level definitions.](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/VHP/alert-level-system) Aviation decisions depend on the actual or forecast ash hazard and applicable issued advisories, including any volcanic ash SIGMETs covering the route. A color code alone does not establish that an ash SIGMET was issued or that a route change was necessary.

## The Vertical Dimension Surface Forecasts Miss

Here is what makes aviation weather its own discipline rather than a subset of general meteorology: the atmosphere a commercial aircraft operates in is not the atmosphere a surface forecast describes. An aircraft approaching Reno may descend through low-level wind shear, icing conditions in supercooled clouds, or turbulence aloft. A surface observation does not describe the whole column. NWS Reno's product published November 11 at 01:04 PST included a High Wind Watch for Surprise Valley, California, and northern Washoe County, Nevada, valid November 13 from 10:00 to 22:00 PST. The Greater Reno–Carson City–Minden area instead had a Wind Advisory for that same window. [NWS Reno wind product, November 11, 2026.](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=NWS&issuedby=REV&product=NPW&format=CI&version=1&glossary=0) Strong winds crossing mountains can produce waves and turbulence, but a surface wind watch does not establish turbulence severity or altitude. Aviation products provide the additional information: AIRMET Zulu covers icing and freezing levels, Tango covers turbulence, low-level wind shear, and strong surface winds, and Sierra covers ceiling, visibility, and mountain obscuration. [NWS aviation product categories.](https://www.weather.gov/zfw/byp_dss) Those products describe different hazards, not interchangeable views of icing.

Pilot reports — Pireps — are the closest thing aviation has to ground truth in the vertical column. When a crew reports moderate turbulence at FL350 over the Sierra Nevada, that observation enters the system within minutes and can trigger or reinforce a Sigmet for the next aircraft on the same route. The data chain runs from cockpit to radio to Flight Service to the Aviation Weather Center, and the latency is short enough to matter operationally. It is one of the few meteorological observation networks where the sensor and the data consumer are the same person.

## Looking Ahead

- Monitor Kīlauea's USGS updates for changes in activity and aviation color code. Assess any ash hazard using the applicable issued aviation advisories and their location, altitude, and validity period.
- Use SPC outlooks to understand the broader thunderstorm environment, then consult current aviation advisories and radar for the route. An outlook does not establish a flight delay or diversion.
- When strong winds are forecast near mountain terrain, check the aviation turbulence and icing products and recent pilot reports. Surface wind products alone cannot specify the hazard at cruise altitude.
