On November 11, 2026, dispatchers routing flights between the U.S. mainland and Hawaii faced a problem that rarely appears twice in the same shift: a volcanic aviation color advisory at Kīlauea and a cluster of severe convective outlooks across the continental West, both active simultaneously. The two hazard types live in different parts of the meteorological taxonomy, but they share the same operational channel — the Significant Meteorological Information notice, or Sigmet — and when both are firing, the workload compounds in ways that surface weather rarely produces.

## What a Sigmet Actually Tells You

A Sigmet is a plain-language broadcast issued by a Weather Service Office or, for volcanic and tropical hazards, coordinated through the relevant Washington Volcanic Ash Advisory Center. It describes a hazard to aircraft in flight — not a forecast of surface conditions, not a pilot briefing, but a bounded geographic and vertical description of something that can damage or destroy an aircraft. Volcanic ash Sigmets and convective Sigmets are issued under different criteria and by different processes, but both land in the same preflight package a dispatcher reviews before signing off on a release.

The Kīlauea advisory current as of November 11 was rated Yellow — the second of four alert levels — indicating elevated unrest without an ongoing eruption. [USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, 2026-11-11.](https://volcanoes.usgs.gov/hans-public/notice/DOI-USGS-HVO-2026-11-11T17:28:12+00:00) That distinction matters. A Yellow advisory does not automatically generate an ash Sigmet, because no ash cloud has been confirmed aloft. What it does is put the aviation community on notice that conditions could deteriorate to Orange or Red — levels at which ash Sigmets become mandatory and trans-Pacific routing decisions get made in real time. Dispatchers routing Hawaii-bound flights on November 11 were not yet rerouting, but they were watching.

The convective picture on the mainland was less ambiguous. The Storm Prediction Center's Day 1 and Day 2 outlooks issued at 1630 and 1730 UTC on November 11 flagged severe convective potential across portions of the West. [SPC Day 1 Outlook, 2026-11-11.](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/products/outlook/day1otlk_1630.html) [SPC Day 2 Outlook, 2026-11-11.](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/products/outlook/day2otlk_1730.html) Convective Sigmets are issued when thunderstorms meet specific intensity thresholds — hail at or above three-quarters of an inch, surface wind gusts to 50 knots or greater, or a line of storms at least 60 miles long. Flights threading through the West on those two days would have encountered active Sigmets on their route packages, requiring either altitude changes, lateral deviations, or both.

## The Vertical Dimension Surface Forecasts Miss

Here is what makes aviation weather its own discipline rather than a subset of general meteorology: the atmosphere a commercial aircraft operates in is not the atmosphere a surface forecast describes. A Boeing 737 on approach to Reno descends through a column of air that may include low-level wind shear in the boundary layer, supercooled liquid water in mid-level clouds where structural icing accumulates, and clear-air turbulence near the jet stream — none of which appears in a standard surface observation. The High Wind Watch issued by NWS Reno on November 11, valid through November 13 at 2200 PST, [NWS Reno, 2026-11-11,](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=NWS&issuedby=REV&product=NPW&format=CI&version=1&glossary=0) signals the kind of strong pressure gradient that generates mountain-wave turbulence well above the surface — the sort of mechanical forcing that produces moderate-to-severe turbulence at cruise altitudes even under clear skies. A pilot reading only the Metar at Reno-Tahoe International would see wind and visibility. A pilot reading the Airmet Sierra and Airmet Tango for the same period would see the icing and turbulence layers that the surface observation cannot resolve.

Pilot reports — Pireps — are the closest thing aviation has to ground truth in the vertical column. When a crew reports moderate turbulence at FL350 over the Sierra Nevada, that observation enters the system within minutes and can trigger or reinforce a Sigmet for the next aircraft on the same route. The data chain runs from cockpit to radio to Flight Service to the Aviation Weather Center, and the latency is short enough to matter operationally. It is one of the few meteorological observation networks where the sensor and the data consumer are the same person.

## Looking Ahead

- Monitor Kīlauea's alert level at the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory daily; a move to Orange would trigger ash Sigmet issuance and force active rerouting of trans-Pacific traffic.
- Check the SPC convective outlooks each morning at 1200 UTC if you are tracking West Coast flight delays — Day 1 Sigmets issued against those outlooks are the proximate cause of most weather-related diversions.
- When a High Wind Watch is posted for the Sierra Nevada or Cascades, expect turbulence Airmets and possibly Sigmets to follow for the FL180–FL350 layer, regardless of what the surface observation shows at the destination airport.
