## Rearview

Thirty tornado warnings in seven days is the headline, and the concentration tells the story: 22 of the 51 storm reports from Kansas alone, with the most active window running from the evening of September 30 into the early hours of October 1 (CDT).

The Lincoln and Russell County, Kansas sequence began at 5:51 PM CDT on September 30, when ICT (Wichita) issued the first of three tornado warnings covering that pair of counties. A fourth warning followed at 6:59 PM CDT, still covering Lincoln County. By the time GID (Hastings) took over the northern edge of the event, Mitchell County, Kansas was under three successive tornado warnings between 7:05 PM and 8:50 PM CDT — a span suggesting either a persistent discrete storm or a cyclic mesocyclone working north along the dryline.

The broader SPC log recorded 24 confirmed tornado reports, 23 wind reports, and 4 hail reports for the week. Texas contributed 13 storm reports, New Mexico 6, and South Dakota 5, pointing to a convective corridor that stretched from the southern high plains into the northern Plains.

Flash flooding was the other dominant story. Of 1,034 total NWS warnings issued, 220 were flash flood warnings and 111 were flood warnings — a combined flood-warning count that exceeded the severe thunderstorm warning total of 101. That ratio reflects slow storm motion or training convection over saturated ground rather than a widespread wind-and-hail outbreak.

Space weather was quiet. The maximum Kp index for the week reached 0, and the strongest X-ray event was a C8.6 — well below the threshold for any HF radio or GPS disruption. Seismically, a M5.9 struck 39 km WSW of Tambolaka, Indonesia on October 3 at 22:55 UTC. A M4.2 near Wauna, Washington on the same day is worth noting for Pacific Northwest readers, though no damage or tsunami potential was reported.

## Roadmap

![NOAA Ocean Prediction Center Atlantic surface analysis.](https://ocean.weather.gov/A_sfc_full_ocean.gif)
*NOAA OPC*

The dominant synoptic feature shaping the next seven days is a sprawling upper-level ridge centered over the Desert Southwest and extending into the Rockies and southern Plains. The ridge is suppressing frontal activity across most of the country, which explains the near-zero precipitation totals forecast for the Northeast, Midwest, Mountain West, and Pacific Coast. The jet stream is displaced well to the north, and the main storm track is running through Canada rather than the CONUS interior.

The exception is the South Plains and the Southeast, where residual Gulf moisture and a weak shortwave embedded in the flow are expected to produce the week's meaningful rainfall.

**South Plains:** Dallas is looking at 2.87 inches of precipitation over the week, with the highest single-day probability — 47% — on October 4. That probability is not high by convective standards, but the accumulation total suggests training or slow-moving cells rather than a single organized event. Anyone managing outdoor operations in north-central Texas or southern Oklahoma should treat October 4 as the primary watch date and plan accordingly.

**Southeast:** Atlanta's forecast carries 1.96 inches over the week, with the peak probability (51%) arriving later — October 10, which falls just outside the 7-day window but is worth flagging. The pattern into the mid-month period looks increasingly favorable for Gulf moisture return and frontal interaction as the ridge eventually erodes. A 51% probability on a single day is a meaningful signal in a region that has been largely dry through September.

**Northeast and Midwest:** Both regions are dry. New York City averages 69°F for the week with zero accumulated precipitation forecast; Chicago matches that high with a 9% peak probability on October 4. The ridge's eastern flank is keeping any Atlantic moisture well offshore. This is a stretch of weather that rewards outdoor scheduling — no meaningful rain windows are expected through at least October 9.

**Mountain West and Pacific Coast:** Denver averages 84°F with no precipitation, a continuation of the anomalously warm and dry pattern that has characterized the region through late September. San Francisco sits at 80°F with a 3% peak probability — effectively zero. The marine layer is compressed, and offshore flow is likely reinforcing the warmth along the California coast.

**International callout:** London averages 65°F for the week, which is seasonally close to normal for early October. No dramatic pattern break is indicated for the UK, consistent with a broader Atlantic circulation that has been running in a relatively zonal mode.
