On March 13, 1989, six million people in Quebec lost power for up to nine hours after a geomagnetic storm collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in 92 seconds flat. The culprit was a coronal mass ejection — a billion-ton magnetized plasma cloud — that had left the Sun three days earlier and arrived with enough force to compress Earth's magnetosphere on the dayside and stretch it into a long tail on the night side. The induced currents that followed were not subtle. Transformers saturated, protection relays tripped in sequence, and the cascade moved faster than any human operator could respond.

That event remains the clearest modern demonstration that space weather is infrastructure risk, not atmospheric novelty.

## What the Sun Is Actually Doing

The Sun's output is not constant. Its magnetic field — generated by differential rotation between the equatorial and polar regions — builds and twists over an approximately 11-year cycle until it becomes unstable. Sunspots are the surface signature of that instability: regions where intense magnetic flux suppresses convection and cools the photosphere to roughly 3,500 K against a background of 5,800 K, making them appear dark by contrast.

When those magnetic field lines reconnect violently, the result is a solar flare — an intense burst of X-rays and extreme ultraviolet radiation that reaches Earth in about eight minutes. Flares are classified by peak X-ray flux: A, B, C, M, and X, each class ten times more powerful than the last. An X1 flare is already significant; the largest reliably measured, the X28 event of November 2003, saturated the detectors used to measure it.

Flares are often accompanied — but not always — by coronal mass ejections. A CME is a separate phenomenon: a structured cloud of magnetized plasma that travels at anywhere from 400 to over 3,000 kilometers per second. Transit time to Earth ranges from 15 hours to four days depending on speed. When a CME arrives, its orientation matters enormously. If its embedded magnetic field points southward — opposite to Earth's northward-pointing field — it couples efficiently to the magnetosphere and drives a geomagnetic storm. A northward-pointing CME largely slides past without effect.

This is why space weather forecasting is harder than it looks. The magnetic orientation of an incoming CME cannot be reliably determined until the cloud passes the L1 Lagrange point, roughly 1.5 million kilometers sunward of Earth — only about 15 to 60 minutes of warning before impact, depending on CME speed.

## How Storms Are Measured, and What They Break

NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center uses two parallel scales. The Kp index runs from 0 to 9 and measures the global disturbance of Earth's magnetic field averaged across a network of ground-based magnetometers every three hours. The G-scale — G1 through G5 — maps directly onto Kp and is the public-facing severity descriptor. G1 begins at Kp 5; G5, the most severe category, requires Kp 9.

The 1989 Quebec storm reached G5. So did the Carrington Event of September 1859, which remains the most powerful geomagnetic storm in the instrumental record. Telegraph operators reported sparks from their equipment and fires in telegraph offices. Some continued sending messages with the main battery disconnected, running on induced current alone.

Modern infrastructure is more capable in some respects and more exposed in others. High-voltage transmission systems are particularly vulnerable because long conductors act as antennas for geomagnetically induced currents — slow, quasi-DC fluctuations that saturate transformer cores. GPS systems depend on precise timing signals that ionospheric disturbance can degrade or corrupt. HF radio propagation, used by aviation and maritime operators, degrades when the ionosphere is disturbed by flare-driven X-ray absorption. Satellites in low Earth orbit experience increased atmospheric drag during storms as the upper atmosphere expands — the same mechanism that contributed to the reentry of dozens of newly launched Starlink satellites after a G1 storm in February 2022.

Nothing major moved on the space weather front this week, which makes it a reasonable moment to understand the underlying mechanics before the next significant event demands attention.

Solar Cycle 25, which began in December 2019, is tracking above the consensus prediction issued at its start. The SWPC's current forecast puts solar maximum somewhere in the 2024–2025 window, meaning the elevated flare and CME rate is not yet behind us.

## Eyes on the Sky

- Check NOAA's 3-day geomagnetic forecast at swpc.noaa.gov before any activity that depends on HF radio, GPS precision, or satellite uplink — storm onset windows are narrow but predictable once a CME is detected at L1.
- Aurora visibility extends to mid-latitudes during G3 and above; a G4 or G5 event can push the auroral oval to roughly 45° geomagnetic latitude, which covers most of the northern contiguous United States and central Europe.
- The Kp index updates every three hours — if you see it climb above 5 on a clear night, get outside and look north.
