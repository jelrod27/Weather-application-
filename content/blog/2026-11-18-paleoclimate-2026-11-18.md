At 3,405 meters below the surface of the East Antarctic Ice Sheet, a 2.7-meter section of ice core extracted by the EPICA project contains air that last circulated through the atmosphere 800,000 years ago. The bubbles trapped in that ice are not metaphors. They are the actual atmosphere, preserved — a direct chemical sample from a world that predates anatomically modern humans by half a million years.

That is what paleoclimate is: a discipline built on reading physical archives that the climate system wrote about itself, long before anyone thought to ask.

## Proxies Are Not Approximations — They Are Measurements

The word "proxy" sometimes implies secondhand or imprecise. In paleoclimate science, it means something more specific: a physical or chemical property that responds predictably to a climate variable and can be measured today. The relationship between the proxy and the climate signal is calibrated against the instrumental record, then extended backward through time.

Tree rings are the most intuitive example. A wide ring generally means a favorable growing season — adequate moisture, warmth, and light. A narrow ring records stress. Dendrochronology, the formal discipline, goes further than width: it uses the ratio of oxygen isotopes in cellulose to infer temperature and moisture source. Oxygen-18 is heavier than oxygen-16, and water molecules carrying O-18 preferentially rain out closer to the equator. The isotopic composition of precipitation reaching a high-latitude or high-altitude tree carries information about where that moisture evaporated and what the atmospheric circulation looked like when it traveled.

![Tree ring cross section — each ring records one growing season's conditions.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Tree_rings.jpg?width=1280)
*USFS / Wikimedia*

Ice cores carry the same isotopic logic. The ratio of deuterium to ordinary hydrogen in ice — expressed as δD — tracks the temperature at the time of snowfall. The EPICA Dome C core uses this signal to reconstruct eight complete glacial-interglacial cycles, showing that Antarctic temperatures during the Last Glacial Maximum ran roughly 8–10°C below pre-industrial values. Atmospheric CO₂, read directly from the trapped air bubbles, ranged between 180 ppm during glacial maxima and 280 ppm during interglacials — until the industrial era pushed it past 420 ppm, a value with no analog in the 800,000-year record.

## Speleothems and the Problem of Deep Time

Tree rings and ice cores are high-resolution archives — annual or even seasonal — but they have hard limits. The oldest continuous ice core record reaches back 800,000 years. The oldest living trees used in dendrochronology extend roughly 9,000 years when dead wood is cross-dated into living chronologies.

Speleothems — cave formations like stalagmites and stalactites — solve the deep-time problem. They grow slowly from calcium carbonate precipitated out of groundwater, and they incorporate uranium as they form. Uranium decays to thorium at a known rate, giving a precise radiometric age for each layer. The oxygen isotope ratio in each layer, combined with the age, produces a climate record that can extend hundreds of thousands of years from a single formation. A stalagmite from Dongge Cave in southern China, published in *Science* in 2001, reconstructed the strength of the Asian monsoon over the past 9,000 years with near-decadal resolution — a record of rainfall variability that no instrument network could have produced.

Marine sediment cores extend the record further still. Foraminifera — single-celled organisms with calcium carbonate shells — settle to the seafloor when they die and preserve isotopic signatures of the seawater temperature and ice volume at the time they lived. Cores from the deep Atlantic and Pacific have reconstructed glacial cycles stretching back tens of millions of years, capturing events like the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum, roughly 56 million years ago, when global temperatures rose 5–8°C in under 20,000 years — geologically instantaneous, and driven by a massive release of carbon into the atmosphere.

![Global thermohaline circulation schematic.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Thermohaline_circulation.svg?width=1280)
*NASA / Wikimedia Commons*

The Younger Dryas, a cold reversal between roughly 12,900 and 11,700 years ago, appears simultaneously in Greenland ice cores, North Atlantic sediment records, and European pollen sequences. That spatial coherence across independent archive types is what transforms a signal into a fact.

## The Takeaway

- Ice cores, tree rings, speleothems, and sediment cores each have different temporal ranges and resolution; the strongest paleoclimate conclusions come from events that appear in all of them simultaneously.
- The EPICA 800,000-year CO₂ record is not a model output — it is a direct chemical measurement of past air, and current atmospheric CO₂ levels exceed anything in that record by a significant margin.
- Paleoclimate is the primary tool for validating climate models: if a model cannot reproduce the Last Glacial Maximum or the Younger Dryas from first principles, its projections of the future carry less weight.
